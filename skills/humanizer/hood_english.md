# Hood English

The rules below come from the linguistics of African American English (Labov, Rickford, Green,
Wolfram), the Multicultural London English projects (Cheshire, Kerswill, Fox, Torgersen), the
work on internet typography (McCulloch; Gunraj et al. 2016), dictionary slang desks
(Merriam-Webster, Dictionary.com, Oxford, Cambridge) and the published complaints about chatbot
slang. Sources are in `research/hood_english_sources.md`.

This register is for text that should read like a real person typing to people they know. It is
not a costume. If the brief does not ask for it, use `generic_english.md`.

Three rules subsume most of the rest. If you keep nothing else, keep these:

1. **One scene, one speaker, one year.** Pick US or UK, pick who is talking, and write as of
   now. A London teenager does not say `finna`; an Atlanta 30-year-old does not say `peng`;
   nobody in 2026 says `on fleek` straight.
2. **Slang is seasoning, not the meal.** Most words in a real message are plain words. One
   marked item per message is normal, two is a lot, three is a caricature.
3. **Grammar follows rules or stays standard.** AAE and MLE are rule-governed grammars. If you
   do not know whether a construction is grammatical, write the standard form. A dropped `is`
   in the wrong slot is the loudest fake there is.

## Scene and speaker

Decide before writing:

- **Scene.** US (Black American and the internet slang built on it), UK (MLE / roadman, London
  and the cities it spread to), or scene-neutral internet (lowercase, `lol`, `fr`, `💀`). The
  neutral layer mixes with either; US and UK street lexicons do not mix with each other.
- **Speaker.** Age and relationship. A 16-year-old in a group chat, a 28-year-old texting a
  cousin and a 40-year-old posting about a game write differently. Older speakers use less of
  the newest slang, not more.
- **Who the reader is.** Slang works because both sides share it. Never gloss a term inside the
  message. People do not write "no cap (for real)".
- **Whose voice it is.** AAE is a community's grammar, and its borrowing as "Gen Z" or "TikTok
  language" is a documented appropriation pattern. Write a Black speaker as a person, not as a
  bundle of features. For a non-Black speaker, the internet layer plus a few words that are now
  general (`lowkey`, `bet`, `fr`) is realistic; habitual `be`, copula deletion and `BIN` are not.

## US grammar: African American English

Every feature below is optional for the speaker and variable in use. Real speakers use them
some of the time, alongside standard forms in the same message. Frequency matters as much as
correctness: sprinkle, never saturate.

### Copula (`is`/`are`) absence

Deletion is allowed where standard English can contract, and follows a gradient: most often
before `gonna` and `-ing`, then adjectives and locations, least before nouns.

| Grammatical | Ungrammatical | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| `she gonna be late` | — | before `gonna`: near-categorical |
| `he working rn` | — | before `-ing`: very common |
| `they outside` / `you wild` | — | location, adjective: common |
| `he my cousin` | — | noun: allowed but least common; use sparingly |
| `I'm tired` | `*I tired` | `am` is never deleted |
| `she was mad` | `*she mad yesterday` | past `was`/`were` is not deleted |
| `that's who he is` | `*that's who he` | clause-final (exposed) position keeps it |
| `he isn't` / `he ain't` | `*he not` as a full answer | stranded negation keeps a verb |
| `is he coming?` | `*he coming?` as a yes/no question in writing | rising intonation can carry it in speech, not reliably in text |
| `it's cold` / `what's up` | `*it cold` in most speech | `it's`, `that's`, `what's` usually reduce to `s`, not zero |

### Tense and aspect markers

| Marker | Meaning | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| habitual `be` | something that happens regularly | `she be at the gym every morning` | `*she be at the gym right now` (that is `she at the gym`) |
| stressed `BIN` | true for a long time, from long ago | `I BIN knew that` ("I've known that for ages") | unstressed use for recent past; in text write `been` and let context carry it: `i been knew` |
| `done` | completed, often with emphasis | `he done ate all of it` | `*he done eat` (takes a past form) |
| `finna` / `fixing to` | about to, immediate future | `im finna head out` | `*i finna went` ; `finna` is not a distant future |
| `steady` | persistent, keeps on | `he steady talking` | as a plain adverb of manner |
| `ain't` | negates `be`, `have`, and past `did` | `he ain't here`, `i ain't seen it`, `i ain't go` | overusing it for every negative |

### Other grammar

- **Negative concord** is grammatical: `i don't want nothing`, `ain't nobody got time`. One
  per sentence is plenty.
- **Existential `it`**: `it's a store on the corner` for "there's a store". Also `it ain't no`
  (`it ain't no way`).
- **Third-person `-s` absence**: `she go there all the time`. Variable; do not strip every `-s`
  in a message.
- **Possessive `-s` absence**: `my mama house`. Plural `-s` absence is rare in AAE and almost
  only after a number (`two dollar`); do not delete plural `-s` otherwise.
- **`they` as possessive**: `they house`. Common in speech, fine in text.
- **`y'all`** is general Southern and Black American; `y'all's` or `all y'all` for emphasis.
- **Orthography of speech**: `gon`/`gonna`, `tryna`, `finna`, `ion` (I don't), `bouta`,
  `imma`. Do not eye-dialect anything else (`dat`, `dis`, `wit` read as mockery unless the
  speaker actually types that way).

## UK grammar: Multicultural London English

| Feature | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| invariant `innit` | `he's moving mad, innit` ; `you went there innit` | tacking it on every sentence |
| `man` as pronoun (I, we, you) | `man's tired` ("I'm tired"), `man like Dave` | using it as a generic "dude" address only |
| `mandem` / `gyaldem` | the group of mates / girls | as a singular |
| `this is me`, `this is them` quotative | `this is him: "where you going"` | replacing every `said` |
| `was` levelling | `we was there` | `*I were there` (that is Northern, not MLE) |
| `bare` as quantifier | `bare people came` | `bare` as an adjective (`*it's bare`) |
| `from` = since/when | `from time` ("for ages") | — |
| `you know them ones` | generic pointer | — |

Do not import AAE `be`, `finna`, `BIN` or copula deletion into UK text. MLE has its own
grammar; it is not American.

## Density and rhythm

- **One marked term per message; two across a short exchange is already strong.** Grammar
  features count toward the budget too.
- **Messages are short and plain.** Most real messages are under ten words. The slang sits
  where emphasis goes, usually the end or the reaction.
- **Reactions do the work.** `💀`, `😭`, `lmao`, `nah` are the most frequent "slang" in
  real chats, and they are scene-neutral.
- **Repeat the ordinary.** Real people reuse the same two or three words (`bro`, `fr`, `lol`)
  instead of showing range. Variety is the tell.
- **No slang in serious turns.** When the content is bad news, money or a real apology, slang
  drops and plain sentences take over.

## Orthography and texting

- **Lowercase by default.** Capitals are for shouting or emphasis (`WHAT`). Lowercase also
  reads deadpan.
- **Drop apostrophes** (`im`, `dont`, `thats`) and most commas. Keep `?` when it matters.
- **No full stop at the end of a message.** A period on a short reply reads cold or annoyed
  (`ok.`). Use it deliberately for that effect.
- **Split thoughts into separate messages** instead of one paragraph with connectives.
- **Letter stretching for tone**: `nooo`, `brooo`, `stoppp`. One stretched word per message.
- **Abbreviations**: `rn`, `idk`, `ngl`, `tbh`, `wyd`, `hbu`, `omw`, `lmk`. `lol` is now a
  softener, not laughter; `lmao` or `💀` is laughter. `😂` reads older.
- **Emoji**: `💀` = that killed me, `😭` = overwhelmed (funny, cute, or sad), `🙏` = please /
  thanks, `😐` = unimpressed. Emoji go at the end, one kind, sometimes repeated (`💀💀`).
- **Typos stay** if they are ordinary (`teh`, missing letters). Do not manufacture them.
- **No em dashes, semicolons, bullet points or bold** in a message.

## AI-impression tells

- Opening with `yo`, `ayo`, `wassup fam`, `hey bestie`.
- Three or more slang items per sentence (`no cap this fit is bussin, it's giving main
  character fr fr`).
- Explaining the slang in parentheses or the next clause.
- Mixing eras (`lit` with `aura`) or scenes (`innit` with `finna`).
- Perfect punctuation and capitals around slang words.
- Eye-dialect spelling (`dat`, `wit`, `ya know`) and fake copula deletion (`I tired`).
- Every sentence ends on a hype word; no plain sentences at all.
- Enthusiasm the situation does not have. Real casual text is often flat.
- `fr fr`, `periodt`, `slay queen`, `and I oop` used sincerely.
- Millennial internet voice passed off as current: `chaotic`, `unhinged`, `I'm screaming`,
  `adulting`, `doggo`.
- Symmetry: a joke, a hype line, an emoji, in the same order every message.

## Usage contexts

**DM.** One to three short messages. Replies answer the last thing said. Slang optional; a
`lol` or `bet` is often all.

**Group chat.** Faster, shorter, more reactions than words. Inside jokes and names. People
talk over each other; not every line responds to the previous one.

**Tweet / post.** One line, lowercase, the joke or take first. A post can carry one sharper
term (`this is so mid`) and a single emoji. No hashtags unless the brief is a brand.

**Voice-note transcript.** Spoken rhythm: fillers (`like`, `nah but`, `bro listen`), restarts,
run-ons joined by `and` or `bro`. Standard capitals and punctuation from the transcriber are
fine; slang density stays as low as in text.

> dj went off fr

> im sorry man. you good?
