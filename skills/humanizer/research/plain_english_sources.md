# Plain English sources

Research run 2026-09-16. The rules in `../plain_english.md` cite these.

## CEFR and learner English
1. Council of Europe, *CEFR Companion Volume* (2020) — rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4. B1 overall reading: "Can understand straightforward, factual texts on subjects relating to their interests or studies"; B2 reads "with a large degree of independence".
2. Council of Europe, *A CEFR Reference Guide for Assessment Tasks: Reading and Listening* — rm.coe.int/a-cefr-reference-guide-for-assessment-tasks-reading-and-listening/1680a9178e. Text features by level.
3. Cambridge, English Grammar Profile — englishprofile.org/english-grammar-profile. Level placement of structures; used for the grammar table.
4. Cambridge, English Vocabulary Profile — englishprofile.org/wordlists. Level of individual word senses.
5. British Council LearnEnglish, C1 grammar: emphasis, cleft sentences, inversion — learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/c1/emphasis-cleft-sentences-inversion-auxiliaries. Clefts and negative inversion taught at C1.
6. Cambridge English, cleft sentences activity (B2 level code b2g058) — cambridgeenglish.org/learning-english/activities-for-learners/b2g058-cleft-sentences. Clefts first appear at B2 receptively.

## Vocabulary coverage and frequency
7. Nation, "How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?" (2006) — lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf. 98% coverage needs 8,000–9,000 word families; 3,000 families plus proper nouns give about 95%.
8. Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, "Lexical threshold revisited" (2010) — files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887873.pdf. 95% minimal and 98% optimal comprehension thresholds.
9. Oxford 3000 keyword list — oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/wordlists/oxford3000. Core defining vocabulary.
10. Browne, Culligan & Phillips, New General Service List — newgeneralservicelist.com. About 2,800 headwords, about 90% coverage of general text.
11. Coxhead, Academic Word List — wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/academicwordlist. 570 families of academic vocabulary above the general core.

## Simplified and controlled English
12. ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English — asd-ste100.org; summary at en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Technical_English. 53 rules in 9 sections; about 900 approved words, one meaning and one part of speech each; 20-word procedural and 25-word descriptive sentence limits; six sentences per descriptive paragraph.
13. ISO 24495-1:2023, *Plain language — Governing principles and guidelines* — iso.org/standard/78907.html. Four principles: relevant, findable, understandable, usable.
14. International Plain Language Federation, ISO standard page — iplfederation.org/iso-standard/
15. plainlanguage.gov / digital.gov plain language guides — digital.gov/guides/plain-language. Common words, active voice, short sentences, you and we.
16. European Commission, *How to write clearly* — op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/bb87884e-4cb6-4985-b796-70784ee181ce
17. European Commission, *Claire's Clear Writing Tips* — alpine-space.eu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/clear_writing_tips_en.pdf. False friends (`actual`/`actuel`), EU jargon.
18. GOV.UK, writing for GOV.UK — gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk. Sentence length about 25 words; common words; no idioms.
19. Simple English Wikipedia, "How to write Simple English pages" — simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:How_to_write_Simple_English_pages
20. VOA Learning English Word Book — learningenglish.voanews.com/a/voa-word-book/6607229.html; history at en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_English_(version_of_English). 1,500 words, one idea per sentence, no idioms, about two-thirds normal speaking speed.
21. Globish (Nerrière) — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globish_(Nerri%C3%A8re). 1,500-word subset; claim of naturalness never supported with published data.

## Translation readiness
22. Kohl, *The Global English Style Guide* (SAS Press, 2008) — oreilly.com/library/view/the-global-english/9781599946573/. Syntactic cues (`that`, full relatives), keep phrasal verb parts together, ambiguous `-ing` forms, noun stacks.
23. TechScribe, review of *The Global English Style Guide* — techscribe.co.uk/ta/global-english-style-guide.htm
24. Microsoft Writing Style Guide, "Writing for all abilities" and global communications — learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/global-communications/. Idioms, date formats, units, `e.g.`/`i.e.`.
25. Google developer documentation style guide, "Writing for a global audience" — developers.google.com/style/translation. Keep `that`, avoid idioms and ambiguous pronouns.

## Readability and simplification research
26. Crossley, Allen & McNamara, "Text readability and intuitive simplification" (2011) — files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ926371.pdf. Simplified texts show less lexical sophistication, simpler syntax, more cohesion.
27. Crossley, Allen & McNamara, "Text simplification and comprehensible input: A case for an intuitive approach" (2012) — journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168811423456
28. Crossley, Yang & McNamara, "What's so simple about simplified texts?" (2014) — files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1031308.pdf. Simplification improved reading time and accuracy; authentic texts use more causal connectives, simplified texts more co-reference.
29. Crossley, "Authentic and Simplified Texts", *TESOL Encyclopedia of English Language Teaching* — onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118784235.eelt0465
30. Yano, Long & Ross, "The effects of simplified and elaborated texts on foreign language reading comprehension", *Language Learning* 44 (1994). Elaborated texts supported comprehension close to simplified ones while keeping native-like input.
31. "The Fewer Splits are Better: Deconstructing Readability in Sentence Splitting" — arxiv.org/pdf/2302.00937. Over-splitting sentences lowers perceived readability.
